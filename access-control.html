Access Control

Who can access a resource, what they can do after gaining access, and why permissions matter in cybersecurity.

CECS 6005 · Principles of Information Security

It is the set of measures used with the purpose of determining who can access a resource and what they can do once they obtain access.

A simple example would be a university student who can enter Blackboard with their account, but that does not mean they have the same permissions as their professor. The student can see and turn in their assignments, but the professor can publish content and place grades.

Access Control does not exist only in computers. It can also be physical. A card to enter a building, a door that requires identification, or restricted access to a server room are examples.

There are two different ideas

Authentication → Who are you?
This function verifies your identity to confirm that you really are who you claim to be. For example:
Username + Password → Authentication
Authorization → What are you allowed to do?
After identifying and authenticating the user, the system determines their permissions. For example:
Student → Access only to student functions → Authorization

Other Concepts We Must Understand

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) means using more than one factor to verify the identity of a person. For example, a password can be combined with a code from a phone or a fingerprint.

Least Privilege means that each user receives only the permissions necessary to perform their work. They do not need to have more permissions than required.

For example, a receptionist at a hospital should not have permission to modify medical records if their work does not require it, since this responsibility belongs to authorized medical personnel.

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) assigns permissions depending on the role of the person on the job. For example:

  • Administrator → elevated permissions
  • Employee → work permissions
  • Guest → limited access

Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC) can make decisions using different attributes, such as who the user is, what resource they are trying to use, where they are connecting from, or under what conditions they request access.

For example, a doctor wants to access a patient's medical record. The system can consider different attributes such as the person's role, department, and working hours:

  • Doctor + authorized department + working hours → ✅ Access allowed
  • Employee + unauthorized department → ❌ Access denied

Why Does It Matter in Cybersecurity?

Let's think about a company with 500 employees where everyone has access to all files, databases, and systems.

Even if all 500 people were legitimate employees, that would represent an enormous risk due to the amount of access available. If a single account were compromised, the attacker could have access to a very large amount of information.

That is why Access Control helps protect especially the Confidentiality and Integrity of information and forms part of a broader security strategy.

Implementing Access Control can help reduce the risk of unauthorized access and security breaches within an organization. By controlling who can access systems and limiting each user's permissions according to their responsibilities, the amount of information and resources that could remain exposed if an account is compromised is reduced. However, Access Control should not be used as the only security measure, but as part of other measures such as MFA, monitoring, security policies, and employee training.
